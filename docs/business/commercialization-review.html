Commercialization Readiness Review

Where the product stands for selling — and a starter kit to close the gap.

Strategy review + go-to-market artifacts · 2026‑07‑05 · Companion to docs/ENGINEERING_MATURITY_ROADMAP.md

The honest headline

The engineering side took a big step this week and is arguably ready to responsibly run a first customer. The business side is still near zero — no pricing, no billing, no legal, no support model, and no evidence yet that anyone will pay. That gap, not more code, is now what stands between you and selling. The single highest-leverage next move is getting one real user in front of the product.

2. What changed this week (the update)

Seven engineering-maturity items shipped, each through the new CI gate. Together they cross a real threshold — from "an app I run by hand" to "an app I could responsibly operate for someone who pays me":

  • FoundationProd/dev separation + deploy pipeline — the prerequisite for having any customer without one edit taking the floor down.
  • TrustCI on every change — technical-due-diligence table stakes; no blind regressions.
  • OpsError tracking + uptime monitoring — the machinery any support promise stands on.
  • DataTracked migration runner — required to run multiple customer databases without hand-surgery.
  • GTMCustomer setup guide — you can now provision customer #1 deliberately.
Net effect

The engineering foundation for a first customer is essentially in place. That is a genuine milestone and de-risks everything downstream.

3. What still needs doing

Technical — correctly deferred (trigger-based)

Building these now would be premature; each has a clear trigger:

  • Dedicated prod hardware / VM per customer — at customer #1 (their instance is born isolated).
  • Containerization / repeatable provisioning — at customer #2, when manual setup starts to hurt.
  • Secrets management (out of plaintext .env) — at the first customer or the pre-sale hardening sprint; every security questionnaire asks.
  • Staging environment — at a second developer or the first customer-facing regression.

Commercial — the actual blocker for "selling"

  1. A pilot / design-partner customer. Highest leverage that exists: validates that anyone will pay, produces a reference, and exercises provisioning for real.
  2. Pricing & packaging. None defined. (Starting proposal in §5.)
  3. Billing. No way to collect money. Add Stripe subscriptions — but only once someone commits to pay.
  4. Legal. No Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, customer MSA, or data-processing terms. Non-negotiable for B2B (templates + one lawyer pass).
  5. Security posture, documented. Good practices exist; nothing written to hand a buyer. (One-pager in §6.)
  6. Support model. Hours, channel, response SLA — what happens when a floor is down after hours?
  7. Onboarding / data import. No path today for a customer to load their parts and machines. White-glove or tooling.
  8. Product identity. Needs its own name + a one-page value prop. (Note: not to carry the Fiserv name.)

4. Recommended sequence (business-first from here)

  1. Land 1 design partner (free or steeply discounted) → validates value, gives a reference.
  2. Name + one-page value prop + a live demo instance you can show.
  3. Draft the commercial basics — pricing tiers, ToS/Privacy (templates), security one-pager.
  4. Stand up customer #1 on its own VM/box using the setup guide (exercises provisioning for real).
  5. Add Stripe billing once someone has committed to pay.
  6. Build data-import tooling (the practical onboarding blocker).
  7. Containerize heading toward customer #2.
The order is deliberate: prove demand and shake out delivery with a real user before investing in billing, containers, or a bigger sales motion. Solo products die by building machinery for customers who never showed up.

5. Pricing & packaging — one-pager (starting hypothesis)

A membership/subscription model priced per site (facility) fits an operations tool better than per-seat (usage scales with the plant, not headcount). Treat these as hypotheses to validate with the pilot — not final numbers.

  • Annual option: pay yearly, get ~2 months free (~15–20% off) — improves cash flow and retention.
  • One-time onboarding fee: $1,000–$3,000 (data import + configuration + training). Signals seriousness and covers white-glove setup.
  • Pilot pricing: free or heavily discounted for the first 1–3 customers in exchange for a reference + feedback — not a permanent giveaway.
Membership-model best practices

Keep tiers few and legible (3 is ideal). Anchor on the middle tier. Bill recurring via Stripe with automated invoices + dunning (failed-payment retries). Offer annual to lock in cash and cut churn. Price on value delivered (downtime avoided, stockouts prevented), not cost-plus. Publish pricing once validated — opacity slows SMB deals.

6. Security one-pager (from your actual practices)

Hand this to a prospective buyer. It is written to be truthful — it claims only what is real today and is explicit about what is on the roadmap. Do not add claims (SOC 2, pen-test, encryption-at-rest) you cannot back.

What is true today

AreaPractice
Tenant isolationEach customer runs a dedicated instance with its own database and its own secrets — no shared multi-tenant data store.
Authentication & accessJWT-based auth with role-based access control (view/edit/PO-manage/user-manage permissions). Single login authority across both apps.
BackupsAutomated database backups (pg_dump), taken and gated before every release, with per-prefix retention. Restore procedure is documented and exercised.
Change managementEvery change is tested by CI before merge; releases are tagged and reversible (one-command rollback); schema changes are tracked, transactional, and forward-only.
MonitoringApplication error tracking and service uptime monitoring with alerting.
Environment separationProduction is fully isolated from development; no developer activity touches customer data.
TransportDeploy behind HTTPS/TLS for any internet-exposed instance (reverse proxy).

On the roadmap (be upfront)

  • Secrets moved from local .env files into a managed secret store (per-instance).
  • Formal encryption-at-rest and a documented incident-response process.
  • Third-party review (pen-test) and a compliance path (e.g., SOC 2) if/when enterprise buyers require it.
Do not overclaim

If asked about something not yet in place, say "on the roadmap, targeted for [trigger]." Honesty survives due diligence; a bluff does not.

7. Pilot / design-partner outreach template

Short, specific, and asks for a conversation — not a sale. Target an operations or maintenance manager at an SMB manufacturer/facility you have a warm path to.

Subject: A better way to track your parts & maintenance — free pilot

Hi [Name],

I've built an inventory & maintenance-tracking system for shops like [Company] — it keeps live parts counts, flags low stock before you run out, manages purchase orders, and ties maintenance calls to the parts they consume, all on one screen your team already understands.

I'm looking for one or two operations to pilot it, free, in exchange for candid feedback and (if it earns it) a reference. I'd set it up for you, import your existing parts and machines, and train your team — no cost, no commitment.

Could I show you a 15-minute demo next week? If it's not a fit, no hard feelings.

Thanks,
[Your name]

Tips: lead with their pain (stockouts, spreadsheet chaos, downtime), not your features. Offer to do the setup and data import yourself — friction kills pilots. Aim for a live demo on a real instance, not slides.
